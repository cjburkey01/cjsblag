# Foreword

Something I wish they emphasized more in math courses is how the different years of classes
actually come together, and why it *actually* matters.

No I'm not going to get into the debate of, "Oh why do we even need math?!" and other such things
you might say sitting in that absolutely mind-numbingly boring and pointless math class. I'm not
going to sit here and blow smoke up your ass, spouting such refrains as, "Hey, you can calculate
the perimeter of your yard!" because we all know that's not why we learn math.

The better reason they tell us is that the act of learning and understanding strictly logical
concepts helps build abstract thought. That, I *can* get behind, but we shouldn't do our students
the disservice of teaching them relatively disjoint concepts that only mesh together when the two
units overlap.

I also won't be going into the, you know, whole education system. I will leave this point here:
**The best kind of learning is led by the learner's own curiosity**. Sure, it's kinda obvious when
I say it like that, but humans are naturally curious creatures. Our brains strive to learn, to
grow, to adapt, etc.; when we are genuinely intrigued, we *desire* an answer, an explanation that
actually satisfies us. I may be-well, I **am**-biased. I love math, so naturally *I* feel this
curiosity should be shared.

That being said, not everyone is genuinely interested in math, and even if we taught it how I
truly wish we could teach it, they might just not really care. **That's okay!** It's important to
have a diverse society! It's fine (and good!) that we have people who just don't want to live in
the world of the abstract, we need people to focus on what truly drives them. Math ain't that for
some (a lot) of people, but I'll still argue that, just as the liberal arts are arguably important
for everyone (that's an essay for another time), math should remain a subject we throw onto
students because it is useful for those reasons that math teacher who wishes their students gave a
damn used to tell us after all.

Anywho, my issue is with how we learn it, so I'm going to just start ranting about how you can get
to calculus, trigonometry, and all that jazz from basic physics principles.

# Let's Get the Ball Rolling

No, literally.

Let's talk about movement, any shape suffices but let's imagine a ball as we tend to have the
intuition that a rolling ball rolls like a ball rolls ball the ball roll.

*![Ball resting on horizontal](ball-on-horizontal-01.jpg)*

This ball right here isn't moving. It's just sitting there, doing nothing. And it's gonna keep
doing nothing, right? I have to do something to the ball to get it moving; I have to apply a
**force**. So I give this ball a shove and...it just keeps rolling. And (assuming there is no
friction and the abstract scene we have built is physically perfect), it will just keep on rolling
until I do something to stop the ball rolling; I have to apply a **for**-well, yeah, you get the
picture. Another force is needed to change the ball's movement.
w
To restate what we've concluded so far, the speed (& direction), or the **velocity**, of the ball
remain(s) the same until a force is applied. So, for now, just think of a force as a change in
velocity. Wow, we've learned Newton's first law. Great job everyone!

Let's continue our joyous journey by imagining this ball just carying along at some speed. How
about 1 meter per second. That's easy to think about. During the span of 1 second, the ball will
move 1 meter. Cool! How about 5 seconds? That's easy, 5 meters! We intuitively know that
$$ 1 \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \times 5 \text{s} = 5 \text{m} $$
